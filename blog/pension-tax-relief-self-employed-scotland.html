Pension Tax Relief for the Self-Employed and Directors in Scotland

By Sardar Muhammad, AAT • Updated 13 October 2026 • 4 min read

Quick Summary

  • Personal pension contributions get 20% relief added automatically by the provider.
  • Scottish taxpayers at 21%, 42%, 45% or 48% must claim the extra relief through their tax return.
  • You can get relief on contributions up to 100% of your earnings, within the £60,000 annual allowance.
  • Unused allowance from the previous 3 years can usually be carried forward.
  • Company contributions for directors are a business expense with no National Insurance.

Pension contributions are one of the most effective ways to cut your tax bill — but Scottish taxpayers often miss part of the relief they're due. Here's how it works for sole traders and limited company directors.

How relief at source works

When you pay into a personal pension or SIPP, the provider claims basic rate relief from HMRC and adds it to your pot. Pay £800 and your pension receives £1,000. This happens automatically, even for Scottish starter rate (19%) taxpayers.

Claiming the extra relief in Scotland

If you pay tax above 20%, you must claim the difference — usually through your Self-Assessment return:

Scottish rateAdded by providerExtra you claim
Intermediate (21%)20%1%
Higher (42%)20%22%
Advanced (45%)20%25%
Top (48%)20%28%

The extra relief applies to the part of your income taxed at the higher rate. You can usually claim for up to 4 previous tax years if you missed it.

How much can I pay in?

  • You get tax relief on personal contributions up to 100% of your relevant UK earnings (salary or self-employed profit) — or £3,600 gross if you have no earnings.
  • The annual allowance is £60,000 of total contributions (yours plus any employer's) per tax year.
  • Carry forward: unused allowance from the previous 3 tax years can be used, as long as you were a pension scheme member in those years.
  • If you've already flexibly drawn pension income, the money purchase annual allowance of £10,000 applies.
  • Very high earners may have a reduced (tapered) annual allowance.

Sole traders

Sole traders pay into a personal pension from their own money. Contributions don't reduce your profit for National Insurance, but they do give income tax relief and reduce your adjusted net income. That can help you:

  • Avoid or reduce the High Income Child Benefit Charge (£60,000–£80,000)
  • Keep your personal allowance, which is withdrawn between £100,000 and £125,140

Limited company directors

Directors can have their company pay into a pension directly. Employer contributions:

  • Are usually an allowable expense, reducing Corporation Tax at 19%–25%
  • Have no employer or employee National Insurance
  • Aren't limited by your salary (but must be reasonable for the work you do, and count towards the £60,000 annual allowance)

For many directors, company pension contributions are more tax-efficient than taking extra dividends. See our director pay guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do Scottish taxpayers claim higher rate pension relief?

Your provider adds 20% automatically. Claim the extra relief (1% at intermediate rate, 22% at higher rate, 25% at advanced and 28% at top rate) through your Self-Assessment return or by contacting HMRC.

How much can a self-employed person pay into a pension?

You get tax relief on contributions up to 100% of your earnings, within the £60,000 annual allowance. Unused allowance from the previous 3 years may be carried forward.

Is it better for a director to pay into a pension through the company?

Often yes. Employer contributions are usually deductible for Corporation Tax and carry no National Insurance, and they aren't limited to your salary.

Can I backdate pension tax relief claims?

Yes. You can usually claim missed higher rate relief for up to 4 previous tax years.

Do pension contributions reduce the child benefit charge?

Yes. Personal pension contributions reduce adjusted net income, which can lower or remove the High Income Child Benefit Charge.
